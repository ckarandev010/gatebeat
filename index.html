A sensor at a door learns to recognise people by the way they walk. Read the one-minute version below, then follow one walk through six short steps. The full study is further down if you want the detail.

The one-minute version

What it is

A door that knows you by the way you walk. The goal is a door that opens as a member walks up: no card to tap, no face scan, no camera. The sensor sends no images; it measures how fast each part of the body moves.

For now, while it is still learning, the pilot uses it only to confirm a face or card.

Where it stands
90%named correctly when it is one of the known members
~11%of members accepted when only 1 stranger in 100 may get through

Good at telling known people apart; not yet strict enough with strangers to be a key on its own. At the pilot door it runs in shadow: it logs what it would decide, and the door still opens on face or card. Every number is from days the model never saw.

Chapter 1 · Topics 1 to 4

Collecting the data

What the sensor sees, how each walk gets a name, what we throw away, and how a walk becomes numbers a model can learn from.

Topic 1Capture
This walk, as the sensor sees it

The sensor streams everything it sees, all day.

A small sensor is mounted at a door at a working commercial site. It sends no pictures. Every frame it reports a few dozen points, each with a position and a speed toward or away from it, plus where it thinks each person is and how fast they are moving.

CarriesSensor linkpasses the stream to the panel unchanged

Sensor data is kept from November 9, when the current sensor went in. Door entries go back to September 11.

For your site: one sensor beside or above the door, with power and a network link. It records movement, never images, so there is no footage of residents or staff to manage.

Topic 2Label
Named by a door entry

Every walk needs a name, and there are two ways to get one.

Recorded sessions

A member walks alone in front of the sensor for five to fifteen minutes while we note the time and anything to cut, such as someone else walking past.

13 sessions over 6 days in November, three members.

Door events

When someone opens the door with their face or card, the panel logs who it was. The walk the sensor saw in the seconds before is that person's walk, once the two clocks are aligned.

778 entries by 22 people since September 11; in a first test, 30 of 47 gave a clean walk.

Everything else the sensor sees is a stranger to the model: couriers, visitors, staff who aren't members. Those walks are the impostors it must learn to reject, taken only from times when no recorded member was on site.

Naming staff walks from door events identifies people by their gait, so it is done only with consent and ethics approval. Today those entries are counted, not trained on.

Topic 3Clean
7 of 10 windows kept

Most of the stream is thrown away on purpose.

The stream is cut into two-second windows, a new one every half second. Then five rules decide what is kept:

  1. Two people, out. Any window within three seconds of a moment with two people in view is dropped. One walk must be one person.
  2. Broken windows, out. Missing values, a speed count that doesn't add up, or no movement: skipped, never patched.
  3. Split into walks. A new walk starts after a 1.5-second gap or when the person turns around.
  4. Approaches only. The door decides on people walking toward it.
  5. Long enough. A walk needs at least four windows, about three and a half seconds.

For your site: a narrow, busy doorway gives fewer clean walks. One person at a time with a few metres of straight approach works best.

Topic 4Describe
One window as numbers

Each window becomes 74 numbers.

A model can't learn from a pile of dots, so each window is summarised. Most of the numbers describe how fast the body's points move. Arms and legs swing faster than the torso, and that mix differs a little from person to person.

The height numbers depend on where the sensor is mounted, one reason a model trained at one spot doesn't carry over to another.

Chapter 2 · Topics 5 to 7

Two models

The simple model we started with, the one we use now, and which of the numbers actually do the work.

Topic 5Neighbours
Its five nearest neighbours

First try: find the walks that look most alike.

Our first door used k-nearest neighbours, the simplest model there is. It doesn't learn anything up front. It keeps every enrolled window, and when a new one arrives it finds the few stored windows that look most like it and lets them vote on who it is.

Why we started here

  • Enrolling is just storing. Adding a member means saving their windows. No retraining.
  • Easy to check. Every decision points to the stored walks that caused it.
  • Small enough for the panel. It ran on the door panel with no training step at all.
  • It worked for one person. It was enough to prove the idea on the first enrolled member.

Where it struggled

  • Every number counts the same. A histogram bin full of noise weighs as much as walking speed.
  • It always has neighbours. A stranger still lands near someone, so it can only be refused by a hard similarity floor.
  • The floor had to be extreme. We needed a similarity of 0.993 and four agreeing windows before the door would trust it.

Pick a window and step through the layers. The stranger's window is the interesting one: its nearest neighbours are real members, because a neighbour vote can't say “nobody”.

Topic 6Forest
Its path down one tree

What we use now: a forest of 300 small decision trees.

A random forest learns from labelled walks ahead of time. Each tree is a flowchart of yes/no questions about the 74 numbers, grown on a random slice of the data so the trees disagree in useful ways. One tree is easy to fool; 300 voting together are steady.

Why we moved to it

  • It learns which numbers matter and ignores the rest, so noisy bins stop drowning out the useful ones.
  • No rescaling. Metres, m/s and shares mix freely, because a tree only compares one number to a cut-off.
  • It can learn “stranger”. Site traffic becomes its own class, so strangers have somewhere to go besides a member.
  • It gives a probability, so each member can get their own threshold.
  • Still runs on the panel. The trained forest ships to the door panel and answers there.

What it costs

  • Adding a member means retraining. The forest has to see them alongside everyone else.
  • It still has to pick from the people it knows, which is why strangers who look like a member still get through.

How the door uses it

  • Classes. One per member, plus “unknown”, built from stranger traffic, weighted so the few member walks aren't drowned out.
  • Per window. The forest gives each window a probability for each member.
  • Per walk. The door averages the windows it has seen, up to eight. It waits for at least four; without them it falls back to face or card.
  • Decision. In the pilot, the face or card says who you claim to be and gait has to agree.
  • Shipping. The model runs on the door panel itself. A new model replaces the old one only if it scores better on the same held-out test.

At the pilot site gait runs in shadow: it logs what it would have decided, and the door still opens on face or card.

For your site: nothing changes for the people using the door while gait is in shadow. Adding a member today means recording them walking, then retraining.

Topic 7Features
What the forest weighs

Which numbers do the work, and did the switch pay off?

Because the forest picks its own questions, we can count how much each of the 74 numbers helps it separate people. Almost all of the work is done by how fast the body's points move. Height helps a little, and is also the part that breaks when the sensor is moved.

  • Speed histogram: the share of points at each speed. The bins around normal walking pace carry the most, where the torso and slower limb swing land.
  • Speed summaries: average speed, spread, fastest points, torso-versus-limb share. The same story told in fewer numbers.
  • Walking speed: how fast and how steadily the tracked person moves. Personal, but it changes with mood and weather.
  • Height and distance: tall people read taller, but it depends on where the sensor is mounted.

Then the fair test: both models, trained on the same walks and scored on the same days neither had seen.

Earlier, with a fixed template per person instead of a trained model, the gap was larger: 41% of members accepted against the forest's 57%, both letting in 7 in 100 others.

Chapter 3 · Topics 8 to 11

How good it is

How we test it fairly, the results, how much walking it takes, and why some strangers still get through.

Topic 8Test
Scored on unseen days

Every number comes from a day the model never saw.

Testing on the day you trained on gives numbers that look excellent and mean nothing, because a person looks a lot like themselves later the same day. So the model is trained on some days and scored on others. Strangers are scored too: every approach on a day a member wasn't on site is a walk the door must not open for them.

Which of two known people is this?

The easier question: the answer is always one of the members.

Is this a member at all?

The harder one: the door has to reject everyone else in the world. This is what decides whether the door is safe.

Topic 10Effort
Longer look, better call

How much walking does it take: to enrol, and to decide?

Two practical questions every site asks. How many times does a new member need to walk past before the door knows them? And how long does the door have to watch someone approach before it can decide? We answered both by retraining and rescoring the forest on held-out days.

Enrolment

More walks help, then level off at around twelve to sixteen. Member B stays low however many walks we add, so for them the limit isn't the number of walks. Walks spread over more days, and a richer sensor signal, are the next things to try.

Decision time

From a single two-second window the door accepts about 40% of members; with five and a half seconds it reaches over 60%. That is why the door waits for at least four windows and keeps averaging up to eight, and why a straight approach of a few metres matters.

For your site: plan for a few short enrolment sessions on different days rather than one long one, and a few metres of clear approach to the door.

Topic 11Look-alikes
Strangers who look alike

Why some strangers get through.

Two things make the stranger problem hard. People don't walk the same way every time, and some strangers simply move like a member. Everything on this plate is an aggregate, and the walk measurements are shown relative to the average walk, never in real units.

You, on a different walk

Two walks by the same person are, in the middle, only a little more alike than two walks by different people, and the spread is wide. In these numbers a different day adds only a little on top of the walk-to-walk variation; the bigger problem is that every walk is noisy.

The look-alikes

The strangers who get in aren't random. They match the member on the speeds of the body and limbs, the very numbers the forest relies on most. A sensor signal that captures more of the limb and step rhythm is the direct fix.

Topic 12Next
The rhythm we want next

Why it's hard, and what changes next.

What we found

  • One person's walks vary nearly as much as two different people's.
  • Step timing can't be read from the current sensor output; we tested it and got chance.
  • Moving the sensor breaks the model, mostly through the height numbers.
  • Real door walks slow to about 1.1 m/s at the panel; recorded walks run 1.2 to 1.4. A model trained on one misses the other.
  • People leaving can be recognised too, 70% against 75% for arrivals, but only by a model trained on exits, and exits aren't labelled in the door log.

What's next

  • A richer signal from the sensor, so limb and step rhythm survive.
  • More people over more weeks, from door events once ethics is approved.
  • A learned model where enrolling someone means storing a template, not retraining. Our open challenge asks partner teams for exactly this.
